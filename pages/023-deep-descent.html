Zone 01 — Surface · 0–200 m

Where the
light lives.

You begin in the sunlit skin of the sea, where the water is still warm and the world is still familiar. Light bends in slow ribbons across the surface. Below you, the blue is already thickening.

Science / 01

Sunlight falls to one percent by 200 m. More than ninety percent of marine life lives in this thin, bright layer.

Zone 02 — Twilight · 200–1,000 m

The blue
goes out.

The light thins to a bruised cobalt, then to a rumour. Down here the animals are small, silver and restless — every night they rise toward the surface to feed, then sink again before dawn.

Science / 02

The largest migration on Earth happens daily: billions of lanternfish, krill and squid climb hundreds of metres after dark, then descend before first light.

Zone 03 — Midnight · 1,000–4,000 m

A darkness
with visitors.

No sunlight reaches this far, yet the water is not empty. It glitters with small advertisements: a lure here, a row of lights there. Most of the light in the deep is made by something hoping to be seen — or hoping not to be.

Science / 03

At 1,000 m the pressure passes 100 bar. Bioluminescence is the common language; an estimated three-quarters of animals here can produce light.

Zone 04 — Abyss · 4,000–6,000 m

Nothing
to stand on.

The abyssal plain unrolls for thousands of kilometres — flat, soft, made of the slow snowfall of everything that lived above. Here, a colony can look like a single animal: a long drifting sentence written in light.

Science / 04

Abyssal plains cover more than half of Earth's surface, yet remain among the least explored places on the planet. Most of what lives here has never been named.

Zone 05 — Hadal · 6,000–10,935 m

The last
quiet.

In the trenches, the water is almost still. Pressure folds the ordinary rules of biology; amphipods the size of a thumb move through a dark that has never known weather. There is no horizon here. Only the long habit of going on.

Science / 05

Challenger Deep reaches roughly 10,935 m. Pressure there is about 1,086 bar — comparable to an elephant standing on your thumb.

10,935 m — Challenger Deep

You have reached
the bottom.

There is no more down. Only the long, slow way back up — past the amphipods, the lures, the lanterns, the last blue rumour of the sun.
