Essay 043 — the mechanics of belonging

The long way
around.

Everything in the sky is falling. The trick, as any satellite knows, is to keep missing the ground at precisely the right speed.

Words by A. Marlowe Illustration: The Almanac Studio Reading time — 9 minutes Set on cream stock

On the fourth of October, 1957, a polished sphere the size of a beach ball crossed the dawn sky above Kazakhstan and changed the weather of the twentieth century. It did not go up, exactly. It went around. That distinction — between climbing and circling — is the quiet argument of this issue, and the reason an almanac still earns its shelf space in a house full of screens.

An orbit is not a place. It is a bargain struck between speed and gravity, renewed every instant. Move too slowly and you return to the ground in a long, apologetic arc. Move too quickly and you leave, an ungracious guest. Between those two failures lies a narrow corridor of falling that never arrives, and every moon and satellite we know is living in it.

The first lesson of orbital mechanics is that the shortest path is almost never the useful one. Draw two points on a map and a ruler gives you a line. Draw them on a turning world and the line becomes a lie: the ground has moved beneath it, the air has other plans, and the destination has been patient enough to travel a little itself. The rocket that saves fuel saves it by going the long way around — out past the trouble, then home on the curve.

“An orbit is a fall that never lands.”

— from the essay, page 13

There is no road in the sky. There are only balances that hold for a while. A planet is not travelling along a groove; it is being persuaded, continuously, by everything else in the room. Jupiter tugs at Mercury. The moon borrows a little of Earth’s momentum and gives it back, monthly, with interest. Even the almanac’s neat columns of times and dates are really a record of arguments, resolved to the minute.

October is generous with such lessons. The full moon rises on the first evening of the month and again on the last, a blue moon by the old counting — two full faces in one calendar, which is only a trick of arithmetic and not a change in the sky. Between them, a new moon slips past unseen, meteors scratch the dark above Orion, and Venus leans out toward the horizon at an angle that has made poets of better observers than us.

To watch a satellite is to watch a negotiation that predates you and will outlast you. The bright point crosses the roof of the house, disappears into the shadow of the planet, and returns on the other side of the sky, punctual and indifferent. Somewhere in a ledger there is a name for it, a number, an owner. But from the garden it is simply a light that takes the long way home.

Perhaps that is why the almanac persists, long after the calendars moved into our pockets. It asks us to look up on a schedule. It insists that the sky has a table of contents, that the month can be read in order, and that patience — not speed — is the instrument with which the largest things are measured. The long way around is, in the end, the only way that leaves a mark.
